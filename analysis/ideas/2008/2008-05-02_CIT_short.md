## 2. 2008 setup
By May 2008 the credit crisis was no longer hypothetical. Investors had evidence of funding stress, asset markdowns and deteriorating market liquidity across finance companies. The analytical challenge was to determine whether CIT had enough liquidity and collateral to survive the gap between maturing liabilities and slowly monetizing assets.

## 3. Company economics under stress
Normal-state earnings can be written as asset yield minus funding cost, credit losses and expenses. In crisis, this equation is secondary to a balance-sheet waterfall:

**cash + committed facilities + realizable asset sales + new secured funding** versus **near-term maturities + margin/collateral needs + operating cash burn**.

If the first side is insufficient, even assets worth more than liabilities in a long-run model may not protect common equity.

## 4. Thesis map
### C1 — reported book can exceed liquidation value
Loan and lease books often carry values based on hold-to-maturity economics. Forced sales during dislocation can crystallize losses well before ultimate borrower defaults.

### C2 — refinancing becomes the catalyst
A maturity wall converts a slow-moving credit thesis into a time-bounded event. The investor should model each quarter's liquidity, not a single annual debt number.

### C3 — government/support optionality
By 2008 policy response became a key source of path risk. Eligibility for guarantees, bank charters, emergency facilities or strategic transactions can materially alter recovery value.

### C4 — security hierarchy dominates
Common equity, preferreds and different debt tranches can produce opposite outcomes from the same enterprise scenario. A debt security bought at a deep discount may be attractive while common equity remains impaired.

### C5 — dilution vs default
Capital raises and liability exchanges can extend survival but transfer value away from existing common holders. Survival is not equivalent to a successful common-equity investment.

## 5. Actual timeline lens
CIT continued to face severe funding pressure into 2009 and eventually entered bankruptcy reorganization in late 2009. The event validates the importance of liquidity analysis, but final investment judgment depends on what security the VIC author actually recommended or shorted, entry price, carry and exit timing.

## 6. Falsifiers for a bearish common-equity thesis
- large, durable government-backed funding access;
- asset sales close to book;
- major capital injection with limited dilution;
- rapid spread normalization;
- maturity extension that removes the near-term wall.

## 7. Payoff tree
**Severe stress:** forced deleveraging/restructuring → common residual sharply impaired.
**Managed runoff:** asset realization covers liabilities → common retains option value.
**Policy rescue:** liquidity improves but dilution/fee burden determines who captures value.

## 8. Postmortem framework
- Business thesis: was credit deterioration as severe as expected?
- Balance-sheet thesis: did liquidity fail before asset value recovered?
- Valuation thesis: did T0 price already discount distress?
- Timing thesis: how long did it take for the funding catalyst to arrive?
- Security-selection thesis: did the chosen instrument capture the waterfall correctly?

## 9. Reusable lesson
A distressed lender should be modeled **maturity-by-maturity and security-by-security**. P/B can look optically cheap for months while the true equity option is being consumed by funding costs, collateral demands and dilution.
